One night, one machine

A computer on a nightstand that does not look away

A Raspberry Pi 5 next to a bed listens to a chest strap, a pulse oximeter, a CPAP machine and a blood-pressure cuff. It judges what it hears in under two seconds, and if breathing and oxygen fall together it sounds a siren loud enough to wake the person it is watching. In the morning the same data becomes an explanation.

II · The 56 seconds at 03:14

Zoomed out of the stave above. The escalation is a ladder with five rungs, and the machine will not skip one. It also will not come back down on silence: only fresh, observed oxygen or respiration can lower a tier.

From radio packet to decision the budget is under 2 seconds at the 99th percentile. Past 5 seconds, slowness is itself treated as a fault and reported.

IV · The machine in cross-section
skin → siren, seven strata

Cut the system open and it is a column, not a web. Each stratum below only ever hands work to the one beneath it, and the width of each bar shows how much of the system’s data volume it carries: millions of samples at the top, one decision at the bottom.

1  BODY
Seven sources on and around one person, each with its own rhythm: heartbeats continuously, oxygen once a second, the cuff on request, the CPAP once a night, the phone hourly, the ring daily, and the journal whenever a hand types into it.
The typed journal is the anchor. Medication doses and how a night felt are the thing being explained; the physiology is context for it, not the reverse.
2  RADIO
One long-range Bluetooth dongle, and only one. Every wireless sensor in the house arrives through that single antenna.
Which makes one failure easy to name: if every sensor goes quiet in the same second, that is the radio, not the person.
3  DECODE
A small program per device translates that manufacturer’s protocol into rows — and writes them in the exact binary format the database ingests, right there at the edge.
Doing the translation once, immediately, means there is no second conversion stage later that can be slow, lossy or wrong.
4  SPOOL
Those rows are appended to a file on the SSD and flushed to the disk itself before anything else happens. This is the line past which data cannot be lost.
Pull the plug one millisecond after a heartbeat is spooled and that heartbeat is still there when the machine comes back.
5  STORE
A drain process streams the spooled bytes into a time-series database on the SSD. Raw signal is compressed after a week and discarded after two years.
Missing minutes are stored as nothing — literally empty — and never filled in with a guess or a line drawn between two known points.
6  JUDGMENT
Reading the live stream, not the stored table: throw out bad samples, score the rest against this person’s own recent normal, look for two sensors that agree, then move a five-rung ladder up or down.
Agreement between independent sensors is what earns the machine the right to make noise. One sensor alone can raise suspicion, never an alarm.
7  CONSEQUENCE
Millions of samples a night narrow to a single fact: sound the siren, or stay quiet. That decision is the only output that has to be right in real time.
Every screen in the system reads the strata above through one query service. None of them can reach into the alarm path, and none of them is required for it.
V · The one idea underneath all of it
Most monitors show two states. This one shows three, and the third is drawn loudest.
Measured, and fine. The quietest mark on any screen.
Measured, and bad. Solid, unmistakable, and the reason the siren exists.
Not measured at all. Hatched everywhere it appears, and always louder than “fine”.

A strap that has slipped off produces perfect-looking silence. Almost every monitoring system in the world renders that silence as calm. Here it holds the machine at can’t assess: it cannot raise an alarm, and — the part that matters — it cannot cancel one either. Missing data never argues that someone is well. Every number the system displays, anywhere, carries the age of the observation behind it.

VI · How it keeps itself honest
A hardware watchdog with a 15-second fuse. If the software stops answering, the board reboots itself rather than sitting there looking alive.
The siren tests itself on a schedule and is believed only when current actually flows through it.
Backups get restored, not just written. A scratch database is rebuilt from them on a schedule, because an untested backup is a hypothesis.
Every sensor has a declared rhythm, so a device that is merely idle can be told apart from one that is failing.
“If this box died right now, what would I lose?” The system keeps a standing answer, in minutes.

Faults about the machine and faults about the person never share a screen, a channel or a voice. A dead oximeter is not a sick patient, and confusing the two is how monitoring systems teach people to ignore them.
