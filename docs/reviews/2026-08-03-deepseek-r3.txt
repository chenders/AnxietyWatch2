    3.3V), the 555 retriggers continuously and never fires. This is a
    single-point-of-failure that a self-test could detect (drive pin low, verify
    it reads low), but the design doesn't specify testing for it.

 6. False negative — software still running but hung: If klaxond isn't panicked or
    dead, but is stuck in a busy loop or deadlock while a thread still pulses
    GPIO, the 555 keeps getting pulses. The system is effectively dead (detection
    isn't running) but the 555 doesn't fire. This is the most likely failure mode
    for a software hang and the 555 doesn't protect against it.

 The 555 is a good idea but the amendment oversells it. The tier-2 independence
 claim ("fires when klaxond panics, when the kernel locks, when the scheduler
 stalls") is only true for stalls long enough that the pulsing thread stops, which
 is a kernel hang, not a scheduler stall or a deadlock.

 ### §4: Revised LUKS behaviour

 The claim: chime instead of klaxon for unlock failure, escalate only after 15
 minutes inside a monitoring window.

 Problems:

 1. "Inside a monitoring window" is undefined: What defines the monitoring window?
    Is it a cron-like schedule? What happens if the Pi reboots at 1 AM (inside the
    window) due to a power bounce? The LUKS unlock fails, the chime sounds, and 15
    minutes later the alarm fires. But the chime might not wake the sleeper. So
    the user sleeps through both the chime and the 15-minute escalation, and wakes
    up to discover the system was down all night.

 2. Race condition: The design says "Never at boot after a clean shutdown." How
    does the system distinguish a clean shutdown from a power bounce? After a
    power bounce, the filesystem was not cleanly unmounted but the OS booted
    successfully (possibly after fsck). The distinction the design needs is
    "user-initiated reboot" vs "unexpected reboot," and that's not reliably
    detectable from system state alone. A flag file written on clean shutdown
    could help, but if the power is cut mid-write, the flag might be corrupted.

 3. The hole: If the LUKS volume can't unlock at 11 PM (inside the monitoring
    window), the alarm fires at 11:15 PM. The user acknowledges, but the volume is
    still locked — the system is blind. The design says the alarm is silenced for
    10 minutes (amendment 03 §5). What happens after 10 minutes? Does the alarm
    re-fire? If not, the system is silently blind for the rest of the night. If
    yes, the user is woken every 10 minutes by a storage fault, which is the same
    "trains you to ignore the alarm" problem the fix was supposed to solve.

 ### §6/§7: Advisory locks + schema fingerprint

 The claim: flock + pg_advisory_lock + schema fingerprint prevents corruption.

 Problems:

 1. Advisory locks are cooperative: Any process that doesn't call flock() can
    still write to the spool files. The locks prevent two well-behaved drainers
    from racing. A root shell doing cat > cur.copybin is not stopped by advisory
    locks.

 2. Schema fingerprint in the spool header: The fingerprint is written when the
    segment is created. If the schema changes after a segment is written but
    before it's drained, the drainer correctly refuses to drain it. What's the
    recovery path? The design says "refuses to drain a mismatch rather than
    corrupting data." Refusal is correct but the rows in that segment are now
    stuck. The upgrade runbook in amendment 01 §2.3 says "drain to empty before
    any major Postgres upgrade" — but what about a schema change that happens
    during sqitch deploy while the drainer is running? The drainer sees the new
    schema, checks the spool header, sees a mismatch, and stops. Now you have a
    spool segment that can't be drained because the schema changed.

 3. The spool_status table: Amendment 04 §7 says "a sqitch change that touches
    samples begins with a guard that raises unless the spool is drained, read from
    a spool_status table the drainer maintains." This is a good guard, but it
    forgets a case: what if the drainer is currently draining when sqitch runs?
    The spool might be "not empty" but actively draining. The guard would raise,
    blocking the deploy. The Conflicts=sqitch-deploy.service systemd directive is
    meant to prevent this, but systemd's Conflicts= stops the conflicting unit —
    it doesn't prevent manual sqitch runs.

 ### §10: Publishing audit chain head off-box hourly

 The claim: publishing the chain head + TPM counter off-box defeats root rewriting
 history.

 Problems:

 1. The window is one hour: Root can rewrite history, recompute the chain, and
    within 59 minutes the published head hasn't yet updated. If the attack happens
    just after the hourly publish, the attacker has nearly a full hour before
    detection. For a system that alarms in real-time, this might be acceptable,
    but the claim "defeats a root attacker" is wrong — it narrows the window to 1
    hour.

 2. The off-box log itself is vulnerable: The chain head is published "to the APNs
    payload and an append-only remote log." APNs goes through Apple's servers. The
    append-only remote log — where is it? What enforces its append-only nature? If
    it's another machine the root attacker also controls, it's meaningless. If
    it's a cloud service, root on the Pi can't alter it, but can stop publishing
    to it.

 3. TPM monotonic counter: The design says to include the TPM counter in the
    published head. But the TPM counter is incremented per ingest_batch. If root
    deletes a batch, the counter sequence has a gap. But what if root suppresses
    the publication of the gap? The gap exists in the local chain but if the
    published head only carries the latest counter value, the gap isn't visible
    off-box. You'd need to publish the full counter sequence, or at least the
    previous counter value alongside the current one, to detect gaps.

 potentially incorrect timestamps, and the incorrectness is worse than collisions

 ### 7. The samples PK (source, type, timestamp) with TIMESTAMPTZ is still wrong

 increasing within a session), but it's absolutely possible for other source types
 like SpO₂ from EMAY, where each sample gets clock_gettime() at notification time.

 timestamp before the previous one, and ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING silently drops it.

 collisions.

 Amendment 04 fix audit

 ### §1 — RR timestamp derivation + PK unchanged

 The PK is still wrong, but for a reason no reviewer raised.

 The claim holds that deriving beat times backward makes them distinct at
 microsecond precision. What breaks is the correctness of those timestamps when
 the clock is wrong. Amendment 04 §8 acknowledges the Pi has no RTC and the clock
 may be 1970-01-01 at boot. The derivation anchors all beats to T = packet arrival
 time from clock_gettime. During the pre-NTP window, every derived beat time is in
 1970. When NTP eventually steps the clock forward 56 years, existing rows in
 samples have 1970 timestamps. New samples get 2026 timestamps. Queries across the
 step boundary return nonsense. The PK doesn't need to change for this — the fix
 is making chrony a hard dependency of the collectors, which amendment 04 §8
 already notes. But the interaction is worth naming: derived timestamps compound
 the RTC problem because an entire night's RR series depends on the correctness of
 a single clock_gettime at notification time.

 Verdict: the PK fix is mechanically correct but incomplete. The timestamp
 derivation must also record the clock's estimated error bounds, or the monitoring
 window must refuse to start until chrony reports synchronization.

 One more thing: connection interval resolution. BLE notifications arrive at
 connection-interval granularity (7.5–50 ms for the H10). A batch of 4 RR
 intervals delivered in one packet are spaced 0.2–2.0 seconds apart in sensor
 time, but their derived host timestamps inherit the connection-interval jitter of
 the packet arrival. If the connection interval is 30 ms and the host's
 clock_gettime is called once per D-Bus callback (which can batch multiple GATT
 notifications), all 4 beats are anchored to a single host timestamp T that has
 ±15 ms of BLE jitter plus BlueZ D-Bus dispatch jitter. The derived times are
 distinct (they differ by RR interval durations) but systematically offset from
 true beat times by the dispatch latency. For CNS detection this doesn't matter —
 RR intervals are what drives the score, not absolute beat times. But for
 correlation with other sources (SpO₂ timestamps from a different BLE connection,
 different connection interval, different D-Bus dispatch path), these systematic
 offsets produce apparent time-skew that looks like a measurement artifact.

 Verdict on the PK: right fix for the collision problem, under-specified for
 correctness.

 ### §2 — OOMScoreAdjust=-1000 + MemoryMin/MemoryHigh

 Does not prevent the failure it names; it moves it.

 MemoryHigh=192M throttles the daemon's allocations but does not stop them. If
 vitalsd has a memory leak, it hits MemoryHigh, gets throttled, but MemoryMax is
 not set — there is no hard ceiling. The daemon can continue allocating slowly
 past MemoryHigh. OOMScoreAdjust=-1000 means the OOM killer selects something else
 — Postgres, almost certainly, since it holds 2 GB of shared_buffers. When
 Postgres is killed and restarted, it must perform WAL recovery. During recovery,
 the spool drainer cannot connect, the spool fills, and if this happens at night
 the 95% disk watermark (amendment 04 §8) may not save you fast enough. More
 importantly: if the memory pressure was caused by Postgres itself (shared_buffers
 is 2 GB, the kernel's page cache is competing, and wayfire is resident), killing
 Postgres and restarting it restarts the same pressure cycle. The design assumes
 the protected daemon is not the one causing the leak, but gives no mechanism to
 detect which process is leaking.

 Verdict: the fix is correct as far as it goes. It makes the safety daemons the
 last to be killed, which is the right ordering. But it doesn't address the
 condition where the protected daemon is the one leaking — it just gives the leak
 a softer landing (MemoryHigh throttling) and sacrifices Postgres, which then
 drags down the spool drainer. A MemoryMax on the safety daemons plus a
 restart-on-throttle in systemd would close this.

 ### §3.2 — 555 monostable hardware dead-man's switch

 False-positive mode: every deliberate reboot, every klaxond restart. Addressed
 above in finding #2.

 False-negative modes the design misses:

 - GPIO pin stuck high — a solder bridge, a failed GPIO driver, or ESD damage
   holding the pin at 3.3 V. The 555 retriggers continuously as if klaxond were
   healthy. The self-test does not cover this: klaxond drives the pin and senses
   current (§5), but a pin stuck high still passes current through the MOSFET. The
   self-test must toggle the pin and verify the level changes, which is a
   different circuit.
 - klaxond alive but detection stalled — a deadlock in cns-engine doesn't stop
   klaxond from pulsing GPIO if klaxond is a separate process (which it is). The
   555 sees pulses, thinks everything is fine, and the alarm never fires. The fix
   is a watchdog within klaxond: if cns-engine hasn't produced an assessment in N
   seconds, klaxond stops pulsing. The 555 becomes a liveness check on the
   detection pipeline, not just the klaxond process. This is not specified.

 Verdict: the 555 is a genuine tier-2 improvement, but the
 false-positive-on-reboot problem is severe and the
 false-negative-on-detection-stall problem is invisible.

 ### §4 — Revised LUKS behaviour

 The hole: what happens at minute 25?

 The design: chime on LUKS unlock failure; escalate to alarm after 15 minutes only
 inside a monitoring window. User is woken, acknowledges (hold-to-silence 3 s on
 the panel). Amendment 03 §5 says acknowledgement silences for 10 minutes while
 holding tier at CAN'T ASSESS. Then what? After 10 minutes of silence, the LUKS
 volume is still locked, the system is blind, and it is still within the
 monitoring window. Does the alarm re-fire? If yes, the user is woken every 10
 minutes all night by a storage fault — the exact "trains you to ignore the alarm"
 problem the revision was meant to solve. If no, the system is silently blind from
 minute 16 to morning, which is a catastrophic failure of the "unknown is not OK"
 invariant.

 The design needs an explicit state: ACKNOWLEDGED-STORAGE-FAULT, which is distinct
 from ACKNOWLEDGED-ALARM. A storage fault that has been acknowledged once must not
 re-fire, but must keep the panel at STORAGE LOCKED (amber) and the system at
 CAN'T ASSESS. The APNs path should carry a periodic reminder (every hour, not
 every 10 minutes). This is a protocol design question, not a LUKS question, and
 nobody has specified it.

 Verdict: the chime-vs-klaxon distinction is correct. The escalation timeout
 boundary behavior is unspecified and the spec has a hole at minute 16.

 ### §6/§7 — Advisory locks + schema fingerprint

 Sufficient for well-behaved processes. Insufficient for what they claim to guard.

 Advisory locks are cooperative. flock on the spool files prevents two drainers
 from racing — good. pg_advisory_lock(0x5641_4C53) prevents two drainers from
 COPYing simultaneously — good. But the claim in amendment 04 §7 is that these
 prevent corruption. They don't. A root shell doing cat /dev/zero > cur.copybin is
 not stopped by advisory locks. The design acknowledges root can do anything
 (amendment 02 §0), so this is fair. But the schema fingerprint guard has a gap:
 if the spool is non-empty when sqitch runs, the guard refuses to deploy. Good.
 But what if the drainer is currently draining when the guard runs? The spool is
 "not empty" but actively draining to empty. The guard must check whether the
 drainer is actively making progress (the spool_status table from amendment 04
 §7), not just whether files exist. The Conflicts=sqitch-deploy.service systemd
 directive handles the case where both are systemd units, but not a manual sqitch
 deploy run while the drainer timer fires.

 Verdict: the locking is correct. The schema guard in sqitch changes is specified
 but not implemented (there is no guard in db/deploy/samples.sql). The race
 between manual sqitch and the drainer timer is unaddressed.

 ### §10 — Publishing audit chain head off-box hourly

 Narrows the window to one hour. Does not close it.

 A root attacker who rewrites history and recomputes the hash chain can do so
 undetected until the next hourly publish. The design acknowledges this ("narrows
 the window, not closes it"). But it misses two things:

 - The TPM monotonic counter gap. If root deletes a batch, the counter sequence
   has a gap: batch 7 carried counter 7, batch 9 carries counter 9. The published
   head carries counter 9. The gap at counter 8 is only visible if the verifier
   keeps a history of published heads and checks for gaps. An hourly publish of
   the current counter is insufficient; you need the sequence. The design says
   "publish the chain head — hash plus TPM monotonic counter." One counter value
   per hour. The gap between last hour's counter and this hour's counter could be
   legitimate (many batches) or a deletion, and the off-box verifier cannot
   distinguish them without knowing the expected ingestion rate.
 - The off-box log's own integrity. The chain head is published "to an append-only
   remote log." What makes it append-only? If it's a cloud service (S3 with object
   lock, Google Cloud Storage with retention policies), it's genuinely append-only
   but requires internet — the system must work when the network is down. If it's
   a local second machine, root on the Pi can't touch it, but there is no second
   machine (gen1-host is being decommissioned, Stage 6). If it's an APNs payload to
   the phone, Apple's push service is the append-only log, and the phone holds the
   sequence of published heads. This is the most realistic option and it's nearly
   specified — "APNs payload" is mentioned — but the phone-side verification is
   not described.

 Verdict: the mechanism is sound in principle but underspecified. The off-box log
 needs a concrete home, and the verification needs to check counter sequence gaps,
 not just the latest value.

 What everyone missed (beyond amendment 04)

 ### 4. Will bite. The tasks and the amendments are unreconciled — a worker
 implementing T-D3 builds the wrong system.

 Already covered as finding #1. Specifics: T-D3 references SQLite schema creation
 in libvitals.c. T-D4 references --db /var/lib/vitals/tsdb.sqlite. T-D5 is
 literally titled "SQLite WAL tail into Postgres." Amendment 01 deleted SQLite
 eight amendments before amendment 04. The task file was never updated. Seven
 references to SQLite remain in the task file. Zero references to the COPY-binary
 spool appear in any task. The Rust crates (crates/vitals-decode/) already
 implement SFLOAT, BP, and HR decoding — but T-D2 specifies C11 with
 vitals/src/sfloat.c. A Rust SFLOAT exists (and is excellent, with exhaustive
 testing), a C SFLOAT is specified in the task but may not exist, and the two
 could diverge.

 Know it by: crates/vitals-decode/src/sfloat.rs has exhaustive 0u16..=u16::MAX
 testing. T-D2's vitals/src/sfloat.c either doesn't exist or is a port of the
 Rust. The tasks need to decide which language the safety path uses, and every
 file referencing the other language must be updated or removed.

 ### 5. Will bite. Source id 0 is both a valid sensor and the SQL INTEGER default.

 VITALS_SOURCE_EMAY = 0 in T-D1's registry. INTEGER DEFAULT 0 in SQL. A row
 inserted without an explicit source value gets source = 0, which is a real sensor
 (EMAY). A row with source IS NULL is caught by NOT NULL, but DEFAULT 0 silently
 fills it. The design says "a source label can never drift" and "no source label
 is ever typed by hand" — but source 0 drifts by being the default. This is not a
 subtle bug: it means a bug in the INSERT path that forgets to set source writes
 data as EMAY instead of failing. The fix is simple: start source ids at 1. Or
 make vitals_source.id start at 1 and reserve 0 as UNKNOWN/invalid. Either way,
 the registry TOML must not assign id 0 to a real source.

 ### 6. Might bite. NTP step after boot creates a timestamp cliff that breaks
 every query.

 When the Pi boots with no RTC, the clock starts at 1970-01-01. Collectors start,
 samples get 1970 timestamps. chrony eventually syncs and steps the clock to 2026.
 All samples before the step have 1970 timestamps; all after have 2026 timestamps.
 TimescaleDB chunks are 1-day ranges. The 1970-timestamped samples land in
 1970-01-01 chunks. The 2026 samples land in 2026 chunks. A query for "last
 night's SpO₂" returns nothing if it uses a 2026 date range, because the data is
 in 1970. The gap-fill query returns all-NULL. The system reports "no data" rather
 than "stale clock." Amendment 04 §8 says chrony must be Before= the collectors,
 which is correct but insufficient: chrony starting doesn't mean it has synced.
 The collectors' ExecStartPre= must block until chronyc tracking reports Leap
 status : Normal or System time : within some epsilon of sanity. A collector that
 starts with a 1970 clock produces data that is technically correct (distinct
 timestamps, monotonic within source) but practically useless for correlation.

 ### 7. Might bite. samples_staging is still in the deploy and verify scripts.

 Amendment 04 §6: "db/deploy/samples.sql drops samples_staging. The drainer uses a
 genuine TEMP table." The actual db/deploy/samples.sql still creates
 samples_staging as UNLOGGED. The verify script still queries it. If the drainer
 uses CREATE TEMP TABLE staging and the deploy has already created a permanent
 UNLOGGED TABLE samples_staging, the drainer's TEMP table of the same name shadows
 the permanent one in its session — it works. But the permanent UNLOGGED TABLE is
 dead code, visible to every other session, and if some other process (psql, admin
 panel, a future migration) touches it, writes go to a table that the drainer
 never reads. A ghost table in a production schema that exists for no reason is
 exactly the kind of drift the design claims to eliminate.

 ### 8. Might bite. The monitor window concept appears in amendment 04 §4 and is
 never defined.

 "Escalates only if unresolved — If still locked after 15 minutes and it is within
 a monitoring window, then the alarm." What defines the monitoring window? A
 systemd timer? A cron expression? A config file? Is it the same as the "Silence
 is an alert condition" window from the main design §5? The main design says
 "During a monitoring window, vitalsd expects samples at a known cadence per
 source." Both the silence detector and the LUKS escalation use the same term but
 describe different things: one is about sensor liveness, the other about system
 availability. If the monitoring window is a single config value used by both, a
 SpO₂ monitoring window (e.g., 22:00–08:00) is the wrong window for LUKS
 escalation (which should fire any time the system is expected to be monitoring,
 which might be 24/7). If they are separate config values, they need distinct
 names.

 ### 9. Taste. The cpap-collector is Python but feeds the safety path.

 Amendment 03 shows cpap-collector in the process topology feeding cns-engine.
 CPAP data (pressure, flow, events) is directly relevant to CNS depression scoring
 — obstructive apneas and pressure drops are physiological signals. The design
 says Python is "not on the safety path" (design §3.1), but the topology diagram
 puts cpap-collector on the same path as polar-collector. If CPAP absence degrades
 the CNS assessment, then a Python process crashing, hitting a GIL stall, or
 leaking memory degrades the safety assessment. Either the CPAP collector must be
 C/Rust (for the safety path), or CPAP data must be classified as enrichment
 (absent without consequence), or the design must acknowledge that one collector
 on the safety path is Python.

 ### 10. Taste. OOMScoreAdjust=-1000 only works if the kernel's OOM killer is
 configured to respect it.

 oom_score_adj ranges from -1000 to 1000. -1000 means the process is excluded from
 OOM killing. This is the right value. But cgroup v2's memory controller also has
 memory.oom.group and memory.max. If systemd's MemoryHigh is set but MemoryMax is
 not, the process can exceed MemoryHigh (it's a throttle, not a ceiling). If the
 cgroup hits the system-wide OOM, the kernel selects by cgroup-level OOM scoring,
 not process-level. The interaction between OOMScoreAdjust on the service and
 cgroup v2 OOM scoring is kernel-version-dependent and not specified in the
 design. On kernel 6.12 (the Pi's kernel), the behavior is: cgroup-level OOM kills
 a process within the over-limit cgroup, and oom_score_adj is consulted within
 that cgroup. So -1000 on vitalsd works as intended unless the vitalsd.service
 cgroup itself is over its memory limit — in which case the kernel kills within
 that cgroup, and vitalsd is the only process there. This is unlikely (the cgroup
 limit is MemoryHigh, not MemoryMax) but the edge case is not documented.

 Architecture wrong, not incomplete

 ### Wrong: The two-backend design persists in code but was deleted in prose.

 The design document, amendment 01, amendment 02, and amendment 04 all describe a
 single-backend architecture (COPY-binary spool → PostgreSQL). The tasks still
 describe SQLite → PostgreSQL WAL tailing. The db/deploy/samples.sql still creates
 a staging table for the SQLite WAL tail pattern. The Rust crate structure
 (vitals-core, vitals-decode, vitals-registry) suggests the implementation is
 moving to Rust, but every C task in tasks.md contradicts this. This is not an
 architectural disagreement to be resolved — it is a reconciliation that didn't
 happen. The amendments describe the correct design. The tasks and the db/ files
 describe a design that was superseded before implementation began. A project that
 prides itself on making "documented rather than structural" patterns impossible
 has its own task file as Exhibit A.

 ### Wrong: node_id without HLC is a UUID pretending to be a clock.

 Amendment 04 §8 removes hlc_physical and hlc_logical ("The HLC is vestigial"). It
 keeps node_id. But node_id without HLC fields is just a 16-byte blob — it
 identifies which node wrote a row, but provides no ordering, no causality, and no
 merge semantics. The design's original justification for HLC was "merge writes
 from multiple independent nodes without a coordinator" — the Pi, the phone, and
 the watch. If HLC is removed, the merge story must be re-specified. What does the
 Pi do when the phone pushes samples with node_id = phone but no HLC ordering? The
 design says "replication progress is a byte offset now; no two writers share a
 key, since sources partition the key space by construction." This is a different
 correctness argument than HLC and it may hold, but it replaces a formal merge
 model (HLC provides a total order with bounded clock skew) with a partitioning
 argument (sources don't overlap, so no merge conflicts exist). Are sources
 actually disjoint? source.healthkit can produce HR and source.polar_h10 also
 produces HR. Different source, same type — so the PK (source, type, timestamp)
 doesn't conflict. But downstream consumers (CNS engine, charts) must merge these
 two HR streams. Without HLC, the merge is by timestamp alone, and timestamp
 correctness depends on both the Pi clock and the phone clock being synchronized.
 The design needs to state this explicitly: HLC removal means clock sync is a hard
 dependency for multi-node correctness.

 Tools, libraries, platform features not adopted

 - ntpd / chrony with rtc support. The design acknowledges the RTC is absent but
   doesn't specify a hardware RTC module. A DS3231 RTC on I2C costs ~$2 and would
   make the "1970 clock at boot" problem disappear entirely. The Pi 5 has an RTC
   header. For a life-safety device that must timestamp clinical data, an RTC is
   not optional — it's $2 to eliminate an entire class of time-related failures.
   The battery-backed RTC also feeds the LUKS unlock scenario: with a correct
   clock, TLS certificate validation works, APNs works, and the out-of-band
   alerting channel is available from boot.

 - MemoryMax in systemd units. The design uses MemoryHigh (soft throttle) but
   never MemoryMax (hard ceiling). A hard ceiling with MemoryMax=256M on vitalsd
   combined with OOMScoreAdjust=-1000 would make the safety daemon's memory
   consumption bounded rather than throttled. If it hits MemoryMax, the kernel
   sends SIGKILL, systemd restarts it, and the 1-second restart gap is shorter
   than a 10-second 555 timeout. A bounded-daemon that restarts is better than a
   throttled-daemon that slowly suffocates Postgres.

 - systemd-creds for key distribution to collectors. Amendment 02 §2.3 mentions
   LoadCredentialEncrypted= for vitalsd's signing key, but the collectors also
   need keys (each producer has its own Ed25519 keypair, amendment 02 §2.1).
   Distributing per-collector signing keys via systemd credentials is the natural
   path (LoadCredential=) and is not specified.

 - rppal for GPIO on the safety path. Amendment 04 §11 recommends rppal — "the
   maintained Rust GPIO crate for the Pi." If klaxond is Rust (as the crate
   structure suggests), then rppal is the right choice for the GPIO heartbeat and
   the piezo siren drive. But the tasks specify C11 for the safety path. If the
   safety path stays C, then /sys/class/gpio sysfs or libgpiod is the C
   equivalent, and rppal is unavailable. Another language-split ambiguity.

 - rustls not OpenSSL for the COPY connection. The spool drainer connects to
   PostgreSQL. If it's Rust, use rustls not native-tls/OpenSSL — avoids pulling in
   a C TLS library with a known CVE surface. The design doesn't specify TLS for
   the drainer connection (it assumes localhost), but if off-box replication is
   added, TLS becomes mandatory.

 Checked and found genuinely fine

 - The SFLOAT decoder in crates/vitals-decode/src/sfloat.rs is correct,
   exhaustive, and well-tested. The 16-bit exhaustive loop (0u16..=u16::MAX) is
   strictly stronger than any fuzz target. The libm_powi10 lookup table covers the
   exact domain. The 0xF14A → 33.0 correction from the T-D2 task brief error is
   documented in the test.
 - TIMESTAMPTZ with session timezone = UTC is the right type. Postgres stores an
   absolute instant; no timezone ambiguity exists at the storage layer.
 - Role separation (§1.4) into vitals_writer, vitals_reader, vitals_owner is
   correctly specified and deferred appropriately (grants are independent of
   data).
 - The WITH NO DATA on the continuous aggregate avoids a full scan at creation —
   documented and correct.
 - The staging-table drain with ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING is correct for idempotent
   replay. The cursor-in-transaction discipline (advance only after commit) is the
   right pattern and correctly specified.
 - Conflicts=sqitch-deploy.service for the spool drainer is the right systemd
   directive for the common case.
 - The dedup counter on the health endpoint (amendment 04 §1.2) is exactly the
   right pattern — making silent loss observable. "A nonzero RR dedup count is a
   fault, not housekeeping" is the correct stance.
 - The tactile channel as redundancy, not physiological advantage (amendment 04 §9
   correction) is honest and well-stated.
 - The flock + pg_advisory_lock two-level locking is correct for preventing two
   drainers from racing — it's the right tool for that specific race.
 - sqitch over Alembic is justified and the verify-script discipline matches the
   project's acceptance-testing culture.
 - The boot-time dtoverlay=disable-bt gate G1 requiring T-A3 green is the correct
   ordering and the correct caution.
